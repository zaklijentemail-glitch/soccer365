**A football formation describes how a team's ten outfield players are arranged, read from defence to attack, and the shapes you will see most often are the 4-2-3-1, the 4-3-3, the 4-4-2 and the two back threes, the 3-5-2 and the 3-4-3.** In the Premier League one of them now dominates: the league's own review of 2024-25 counted 408 uses of the 4-2-3-1, more than the next three shapes combined.

This page compares every common formation side by side, shows how they evolved, and links to a full guide on each one.

## How to read a formation

Law 3 of the [Laws of the Game](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/the-players/) says a match is played by two teams "each with a maximum of eleven players; one must be the goalkeeper". The goalkeeper is left out of the numbers, so the digits always add up to ten.

The digits run from the back. A **4-4-2** is four defenders, four midfielders and two forwards. When a line is split into layers, a fourth number appears: a **4-2-3-1** is a back four, two holding midfielders, three attacking midfielders and one striker. Within each line, players are read left to right.

Two things the numbers do not tell you. First, they describe a starting position, not where players stand once the ball moves: almost every shape becomes a different one without the ball. Second, they say nothing about the roles inside a line. A front two might be a [target man and a poacher](/guides/types-of-striker-explained), and a back four might hold an [inverted full-back](/guides/fullback-vs-wingback-vs-inverted-fullback). The same numbers can hide very different teams.

## The back-four shapes

### 4-4-2

<Formation shape="4-4-2" roles="GK,LB,CB,CB,RB,LM,CM,CM,RM,ST,ST" caption="The flat 4-4-2: two banks of four and a strike pair" />

The Premier League's [4-4-2 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4243787) traces it to the Russian coach Viktor Maslov, who developed a version of the 4-2-4 "in which the two wingers dropped back", and who "led Dynamo Kyiv to three consecutive league titles in the late 1960s". It names Sir Alex Ferguson's Manchester United and Diego Simeone's Atlético Madrid among its best-known users.

Its case is simple: it "offers a strong defensive presence across the entire pitch" and "provides the perfect balance for counter-attacking football". Its problem is the middle, where teams in a 4-4-2 "can face overloads in central midfield".

**The diamond** keeps the back four and the strike pair but narrows the midfield into a holder, two shuttlers and a No.10. The league's [diamond explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4243786/the-4-4-2-diamond-football-tactics-explained) calls its main strength "the overload it creates in central midfield" and its weakness plainly: it "lacks natural width in attack". Massimiliano Allegri turned Juventus from a 3-5-2 into a diamond in his first season there.

### 4-3-3

<Formation shape="4-3-3" roles="GK,LB,CB,CB,RB,CM,DM,CM,LW,ST,RW" lift="DM:-0.3" caption="The 4-3-3: one holder, two No.8s, wingers level with the striker" />

The front three is three forwards, typically two wingers and a striker, and the Premier League's [4-3-3 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4243812) says the system "thrives on wide play" and "lends itself to rapid counter-attacking". It cites Mikel Arteta's Arsenal, Xavi's Barcelona and Jürgen Klopp's Liverpool as high-profile users. The weak point is the press: if the three forwards are bypassed, "the first line of the press can be vulnerable".

### 4-2-3-1

<Formation shape="4-2-3-1" roles="GK,LB,CB,CB,RB,DM,CM,LW,AM,RW,ST" caption="The 4-2-3-1: a double pivot behind a No.10 and two wide attackers" />

The most-used shape in England today is a product of this century. The league's [4-2-3-1 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4243785) says it "was eventually introduced in the 2000s to prevent access into central spaces between the lines", and that Juanma Lillo "is thought to have instigated the change from 4-4-2 to 4-2-3-1 in Spanish football". Hansi Flick's Bayern Munich won the 2019-20 Champions League in it.

It "provides a central midfield three that can overload a midfield two" and "lends itself to triangles". Against it: "the single centre-forward can be isolated", and a midfield four such as a diamond can overload its three.

### 4-1-4-1

Less a starting formation than a way of defending. The league's [4-1-4-1 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4243851) says it "often becomes a defensive component of the 4-3-3", with the wingers dropping in line with the midfielders, and that it "provides the foundation for a very effective low or mid-block". Klopp's Liverpool often used it to defend just inside the opposition half. The cost is up front, where the lone striker "can be isolated and easily overloaded when beginning a counter-attack".

## The back-three shapes

A back three plays three centre-backs and hands all of the width to two wing-backs. Without the ball those wing-backs drop, and the side defends with five across. Both shapes depend on [centre-backs](/guides/centre-back-role-explained) comfortable defending wide areas.

### 3-5-2

<Formation shape="3-5-2" roles="GK,LCB,CB,RCB,LWB,CM,DM,CM,RWB,ST,ST" lift="LWB:0.35,RWB:0.35" caption="The 3-5-2: three centre-backs, wing-backs pushed on, three in midfield, a front two" />

The league's [3-5-2 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4244123) says "it was Antonio Conte who brought the 3-5-2 back into vogue during his successful time at Juventus", and that Simone Inzaghi's Inter lined up in it for the 2023 Champions League final. It calls "attacking versatility" its main benefit and notes that the back three "usually provides an overload in the build-up". The price is paid on the flanks: the system "places high demands on the wing-backs".

### 3-4-3 and 3-4-2-1

<Formation shape="3-4-2-1" roles="GK,LCB,CB,RCB,LWB,CM,CM,RWB,AM,AM,ST" lift="LWB:0.3,RWB:0.3" caption="The 3-4-2-1: a double pivot, wing-backs high, two No.10s behind a striker" />

Swap a central midfielder from the 3-5-2 for a third forward and you have a 3-4-3. The league's [3-4-3 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4244194) says it "provides good balance between attack and defence", committing five outfield players to attacks while the other five cover. Conte won the Premier League and the FA Cup with Chelsea using it. Its weakness is the space a back three leaves: "opposition counter-attacks can target wide areas, as the back line of three covers less space" than a back four.

## What Premier League teams actually play

The league counts every starting formation each season: 380 matches, two line-ups each. Its [review of 2024-25](https://www.premierleague.com/news/4323030) shows how far one shape has pulled away.

| Formation | 2023-24 uses | 2024-25 uses | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4-2-3-1 | 296 | 408 | +112 |
| 3-4-2-1 | 71 | 117 | +46 |
| 4-3-3 | 172 | 114 | -58 |
| 4-1-4-1 | not listed | 27 | — |
| 4-4-2 (flat) | 54 | 20 | -34 |
| 4-4-1-1 | 41 | not listed | — |
| 3-5-2 | 41 | not listed | — |

Three trends stand out. The 4-2-3-1 became the primary shape of 13 of the 20 clubs, and Andoni Iraola's Bournemouth and Enzo Maresca's Chelsea used it in all 38 league games. The 3-4-2-1 was the biggest climber, favoured by Oliver Glasner's Crystal Palace, Ruben Amorim's Manchester United and Vítor Pereira's Wolves. And the review noted that "the usually popular 4-3-3 has gone a little out of style", with Arteta, Eddie Howe and Ange Postecoglou its main remaining users.

The flat 4-4-2, the shape behind Ferguson's Manchester United, started just 20 times all season.

## How the shapes evolved

| Era | Shape | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| By the 1890s | 2-3-5 | The standard shape in England: two defenders, three half-backs, five forwards |
| 1925 | Offside law | Attackers now needed two opponents, not three, between them and the goal line |
| From 1925 | W-M (3-2-2-3) | Herbert Chapman's Arsenal dropped the centre-half into defence as a third back |
| 1958 | 4-2-4 | Brazil won their first World Cup with two high, wide wingers beside the front two |
| Late 1960s | 4-4-2 | Viktor Maslov's Dynamo Kyiv dropped the 4-2-4's wingers into midfield |
| 1970 | 4-2-4 variant | Mário Zagallo, a member of the 1958 side, coached Brazil to the title |
| 2000s | 4-2-3-1 | Introduced to deny space between the lines; Juanma Lillo thought to have led the switch in Spain |
| 2024-25 | 4-2-3-1 | Primary shape of 13 of the 20 Premier League clubs |

The 1925 change is the clearest example of a law reshaping tactics. Arsenal's own history records that after the change "only two defenders (it was previously three) needed to be between an attacker and the goal", and that a [7-0 defeat at Newcastle](http://www.arsenal.com/news/why-7-0-loss-newcastle-led-our-golden-era) in late September 1925 convinced Chapman and his captain Charlie Buchan to withdraw centre-half Jack Butler into the defence. That move created the W-M, which the Premier League's 3-4-3 explainer credits as the first consistent back three. The modern version of the law is set out in [offside explained simply](/guides/offside-explained-simply).

Most of the big shifts since have been answers to the shape before. The 4-4-2 answered the 4-2-4 by putting more bodies in midfield; the 4-2-3-1 answered the withdrawn forward of the late-1990s 4-4-2 by filling the space between the lines.

## One team, several shapes

A formation is a starting point, and most teams play at least two in every match. The league's explainers give clear examples: a 4-3-3 commonly defends as a 4-1-4-1 when the wingers drop, and a 3-5-2 can press in "a pressing 4-4-2 block" without the ball. Back-three sides retreat into a five when the wing-backs come back.

Coaches also switch between games and within them. In 2024-25 Thomas Frank and Nuno Espírito Santo each used seven different formations in the Premier League, and only Iraola and Maresca stuck to one shape all season. Each change of shape is limited by how many changes a coach is allowed: the numbers are in [football substitution rules](/guides/football-substitution-rules-explained).

## How to choose between them

The explainers above point to a few questions that decide most choices.

**Where are the best players?** Two good strikers point to a 4-4-2 or 3-5-2. Strong wingers point to a 4-3-3. A gifted No.10 points to a 4-2-3-1 or a diamond.

**Who wins the middle?** A two-man midfield is outnumbered by a three, and a three can be outnumbered by a diamond's four. Much of tactical planning is counting heads in central midfield.

**Who covers the width?** Back fours lean on full-backs and wingers. Back threes lean on two wing-backs, who need the stamina to cover a whole flank.

**How does the team want to defend?** Sides expecting to spend long spells without the ball favour compact shapes such as the 4-4-2, the 4-1-4-1 or a back five. Sides that press high want three forwards, as in the 4-3-3 or 3-4-3, and often a [sweeper keeper](/guides/sweeper-keeper-explained) to cover the space behind.

There is no best formation, only the right one for a squad and an opponent. The guides linked in the comparison table go through each shape player by player.
