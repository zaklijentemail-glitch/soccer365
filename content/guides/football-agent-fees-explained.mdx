Agent fees are the part of football finance most people find hardest to understand, and the part clubs are least keen to discuss. Here's how the money actually moves.

## What an agent is for

The caricature is a man on a phone inflating prices. The actual job is broader, and most of it isn't transfers at all.

An agent negotiates contracts — new ones, renewals, bonus structures, image rights. He finds clubs when a player needs one and fields approaches when he doesn't. He handles the parts of a young professional's life that a 19-year-old has no way of handling alone: tax, relocation, sponsorship, occasionally legal trouble. And he manages the player's relationship with a club that has far more power than the player does.

That last point is the honest case for agents existing. A footballer negotiating alone with an institution that employs lawyers full-time is not a fair negotiation. The counter-case is equally honest: the money involved is enormous, the transparency is poor, and the incentives don't always point where they should.

## Who actually pays

There's no single answer, and that's the source of most of the confusion.

**The player pays.** The straightforward arrangement: the agent works for the player and takes a percentage of what the player earns. Commission is usually a share of gross salary across the contract, paid annually rather than upfront.

**The buying club pays.** Extremely common. The club signing the player pays the player's agent directly, on the reasoning that the agent delivered the signing. Clubs prefer this because the payment is a club expense rather than money out of the player's wages — and the player prefers it for the same reason.

**The selling club pays.** Happens where an agent has brokered a sale the club wanted, effectively acting for the seller.

**More than one of them pays.** Which brings us to the contested part.

## Dual representation

**Dual representation** is when one agent is paid by both sides of the same transaction — most often by the player and by the club buying him.

The problem is easy to state. The agent's job for the player is to extract the best possible terms from the club. If that same club is also paying him, his interest in a hard negotiation is compromised. He's on both sides of a table where the two sides want opposite things.

It's permitted in various forms, generally requiring the written consent of everyone involved and disclosure to the governing body. But consent from a young player who wants the move to happen isn't worth a great deal, and this remains one of the sharpest criticisms of how football business is done.

Regulators have moved against it repeatedly. Enforcement, and what exactly is allowed, differs by jurisdiction.

## Why free transfers generate the biggest fees

This surprises people, but it's a direct consequence of the arithmetic.

In a normal transfer, a large sum goes to the selling club. On a free transfer nobody is owed a fee — and the money the buying club had available doesn't evaporate. It gets redistributed to the parties still in the deal: the player, in salary and signing-on fees, and the agent, in commission.

An agent who delivers a top player at no transfer cost has produced something of enormous value to the club, and is paid accordingly. This is why the largest single agent payments in football have tended to come from out-of-contract moves rather than record transfers. Our guide to [free transfers and the Bosman ruling](/guides/free-transfers-and-the-bosman-ruling) covers why those deals became possible in the first place.

## What commission is calculated on

Two different bases, and the difference matters:

- **A percentage of the transfer fee.** Paid once, tied to the size of the deal. This aligns the agent with a *large fee* — which is the selling club's interest, not always the player's.
- **A percentage of the player's wages** over the life of the contract. Paid across the term, and aligns the agent with the player's earnings.

Contracts frequently include both, plus payments triggered by add-ons: appearances, promotion, qualification, renewal. An agent's income from one player can run for years after the move itself.

Clubs also pay agents for work that isn't a transfer at all — a contract renewal is a negotiation like any other, and commission is typically payable on it. This is one reason a club can pay substantial agent fees in a window where it signed nobody.

## The regulation fight

Football has tried to bring agent fees under control repeatedly, and it has been messy.

FIFA introduced a framework covering **licensing** (agents must pass an exam and be registered), **disclosure** (deals must be reported to a central system), **restrictions on dual representation**, and **caps on commission** as a percentage of the deal.

The caps in particular ran into immediate legal resistance. Agents challenged them in national courts and under competition law, arguing that a global limit on what an intermediary may charge is a restraint of trade. Parts of the framework were suspended or disapplied in some jurisdictions while cases proceeded, and the picture has shifted more than once.

The practical consequence for a reader: **don't assume a fixed cap applies**. What binds an agent depends on where the deal is done and what the current state of litigation is in that country. Governing bodies publish the applicable regulations, and those are the place to check rather than any figure quoted in a news story.

## Transparency, and what's still missing

Several leagues now publish annual totals of what clubs paid to agents. Those figures are consistently large enough to reframe how you read a transfer window: a club that appears to have spent modestly on players may have spent heavily on intermediaries.

What's usually still absent is the breakdown — which deal, which agent, paid by whom, and whether the same agent was paid by both sides. Aggregate disclosure tells you the scale. It doesn't tell you whether any individual transaction was clean.

## What this means when you read transfer news

- **"The deal collapsed over agent fees"** is not a footnote. Commission is a real line item and deals genuinely die on it.
- **A club paying a fee, wages and commission** is committing far more than the reported transfer figure. The headline number is never the cost.
- **"His agent is pushing for a move"** describes someone with a direct financial interest in the move happening. That doesn't make it wrong, but it's worth knowing.
- **A quiet window with big agent spending** usually means renewals, not transfers.
