You've watched the clock hit 90 minutes, but the match keeps going. A number flashes up on a board: +7. Nobody explains why. This guide breaks down exactly where that number comes from.

## The basic idea

A football match is 90 minutes long, split into two 45-minute halves. But the clock never stops for things like injuries, substitutions or goal celebrations, the way it does in sports like basketball.

To make up for that lost time, the referee adds extra minutes to the end of each half. That's stoppage time, also called added time or injury time. It's the game's way of making sure 90 minutes of actual football gets played, not 90 minutes of clock time that included pauses.

## How the number gets calculated

The referee is the one keeping track, either mentally or with a stopwatch, adding up time lost during the half for things like:

- Injuries and physio treatment on the pitch.
- Substitutions, since players have to jog off and on.
- Goal celebrations, which can eat up a minute or more each.
- VAR reviews, when the referee checks a decision on the pitch-side monitor.
- Time-wasting, like a goalkeeper holding the ball too long or slow free kicks.

Near the end of the half, the fourth official holds up an electronic board showing the referee's total. That's the signal to players, coaches and fans for roughly how much longer the half will run.

## The number on the board is a minimum, not a deadline

This is the part that trips people up most. The board isn't a countdown to a hard stop. It's the referee's estimate at that moment, based on stoppages so far.

If anything else stops play after the board goes up, another injury, another VAR check, another goal and its celebration, the referee simply keeps adding time. That's why a match can run several minutes past the number that was shown.

## Why added time keeps getting longer

If you feel like stoppage time used to be a lazy "2 or 3 minutes" and now regularly hits 8, 10 or even 12 minutes, that's not in your head. Governing bodies like FIFA have pushed referees to track stoppages far more strictly, particularly time lost to goal celebrations and substitutions in the closing stages of tight games.

The goal is simple: stop teams from running down the clock late in a match and make sure fans actually get the full 90 minutes of play they paid for.

## A simple example

Say the first half has:

- A 2-minute injury stoppage.
- A substitution that takes 1 minute.
- A goal celebration that runs 1 minute.

That's 4 minutes lost to stoppages, so the fourth official would show roughly +4. The half would then run until the 49th minute, unless something else happens in that added time to push it further.
