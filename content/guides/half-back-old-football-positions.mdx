**A half-back was a midfielder: one of the three players in football's old 2-3-5 formation who stood between the two full-backs and the five forwards.** They were called the left-half, the centre-half and the right-half, and between them they were the first line to meet the opposing attack. The name faded once the shapes changed, and by the early 1970s "midfield" had replaced it.

Most of the old position names did not disappear so much as move. The centre-half walked back into defence and became the centre-back. The wing-halves became the midfield. The inside-forwards became playmakers and second strikers. This page shows where each one went, and why.

## The 2-3-5: where the names come from

According to the [history of football formations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formation_(association_football)), "the first long-term successful formation was recorded in 1880", and "by the 1890s, it was the standard formation in England and had spread all over the world". Wrexham, the first winners of the Welsh Cup in 1877, are recorded as having played "three half-backs and five forwards".

It was known at the time as the Pyramid. Read from the back, it was two full-backs, three half-backs and five forwards.

<Formation shape="2-3-5" roles="GK,LB,RB,LH,CH,RH,OL,IL,CF,IR,OR" caption="The 2-3-5 Pyramid: two full-backs, three half-backs, five forwards" />

Every name tells you where the player stood. The "backs" were behind; the "full" backs were the furthest back of all, and the "half" backs were half-way between them and the forwards. In the [standard naming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football_positions) of the shape, the defenders were "full-backs, known as the left-back and right-back", the midfield was the left-half, centre-half and right-half, and the forward line ran outside-left, inside-left, centre-forward, inside-right, outside-right.

The half-backs were the hinge. In the 2-3-5, "the halfback-trio were the first facing opposing forwards; when those were surpassed, then fullbacks met forwards". The centre-half was the most important of the three: he "had a key role in both helping to organise the team's attack and marking the opponent's centre forward".

## Every old position and its modern name

| Old position | Where it played in the 2-3-5 | What it became |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-back (left-back, right-back) | Last line in front of the goalkeeper, centrally | The wide defenders of a back four |
| Centre-half | Middle of the half-back line, marking the centre-forward | Centre-back |
| Wing-half (left-half, right-half) | Outer half-backs | Central and wide midfielders |
| Inside-forward (inside-left, inside-right) | Either side of the centre-forward | Second striker, playmaker, No.10 |
| Outside-forward (outside-left, outside-right) | Wide on the forward line | Winger |
| Centre-forward | Point of the attack | Striker, the No.9 |

The table follows the [positions reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_football_positions), which notes that "originally, a full-back was the last line of defence", and that once the centre-half dropped back the full-backs "were pushed wider to form the right-back and left-back positions". The modern full-back, and the wing-back and inverted full-back that grew out of it, are set out in [full-back vs wing-back vs inverted full-back](/guides/fullback-vs-wingback-vs-inverted-fullback).

## 1925: the change that moved the centre-half

The 2-3-5 was built around an offside law that needed three opponents between an attacker and the goal line. In the close-season of 1925 that dropped to two. Arsenal's own history records that after the change "only two defenders (it was previously three) needed to be between an attacker and the goal".

[Arsenal's account](https://www.arsenal.com/feature/why-a-7-0-loss-at-newcastle-led-to-our-golden-era-ajy5Q5Z8Lbbg) of what happened next centres on a 7-0 defeat at Newcastle United in late September 1925, in which Arsenal were 6-0 down at half-time. Herbert Chapman, the new manager, and his captain Charlie Buchan decided the side had to change. Buchan later wrote: "New methods were required and Arsenal were the first to exploit them."

Their answer was to take the centre-half out of midfield. Jack Butler was the first player asked to play the deep-lying centre-half role, "a role that became known as centre-back", and Herbie Roberts held it once the system was running smoothly at the start of the 1930s. The new centre-half was to:

- guard the edge of the penalty area rather than chase the centre-forward around the pitch;
- take charge of the offside trap, a job that had belonged to the full-backs;
- be, in the club's words, "a dominating personality around his own goal".

The modern version of the law that started all this is in [offside explained simply](/guides/offside-explained-simply).

## The W-M: half-backs become a midfield pair

Dropping the centre-half between the two full-backs gave a back three. With the two inside-forwards sitting behind a front three, the 2-3-5 became a 3-2-2-3: the W-M. Chapman's version "included a centre-back to stop the opposing centre-forward".

<Formation shape="3-2-2-3" roles="GK,LB,CH,RB,LH,RH,IL,IR,OL,CF,OR" caption="The W-M: the centre-half in the back three, the wing-halves as a midfield pair, the inside-forwards behind the front three" />

Look at what happened to each name. The centre-half was now a defender but kept his name for a while; centre-half later became centre-back. The two wing-halves became the two-man midfield in front of the back three. The inside-forwards became creators linking midfield and attack; at Arsenal, the club notes, Alex James played that role in the early 1930s, supplying the wingers Joe Hulme and Cliff Bastin.

The formations reference credits the system's shape with a specific advantage: "The gap in the centre of the formation between the two wing halves (Half backs) and the two inside forwards allowed Arsenal to counter-attack effectively." The Premier League's [3-4-3 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4244194) calls the W-M "the first example of a back three being used consistently".

It worked. Chapman's Arsenal won the club's [first major trophy](https://www.arsenal.com/feature/herbert-chapmans-arsenal-100-years-on-aASl10x5cjTg), the FA Cup, at Wembley on 26 April 1930, and the league title in 1930-31, losing only four of 42 league games. A second title followed in 1932-33. [Chapman died](https://www.arsenal.com/feature/herbert-chapman-the-great-innovator-adKqw1D6SGK3) at his home in Hendon in the early hours of 6 January 1934, aged 55, and Arsenal won the league that season under the caretaker Joe Shaw.

## Why centre-backs wear No.5

The old positions survive most clearly on the back of the shirt. Arsenal and Chelsea wore numbers in the Football League for the first time on 25 August 1928, and the [Football League made them compulsory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_(sports)) from the 1939-40 season.

Numbers were handed out by position, and the system "came about based on the traditional 2–3–5": the goalkeeper wore 1, the full-backs 2 and 3, the half-backs 4, 5 and 6, and the forwards 7 to 11. When the W-M moved "the centre half back to become a defender rather than a half back", the [squad-number history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squad_number_(association_football)) notes, "their numbers went with them, hence central defenders wearing number 5". The fixed 1-to-11 system lasted in England until squad numbers arrived in 1993.

## How the W-M was broken: Hungary 1953

The W-M's centre-half was built to mark one man: the opposing centre-forward. That made him predictable. At Wembley on 25 November 1953, in front of 105,000 spectators, Hungary beat England 6-3 using what the [match record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England_v_Hungary_(1953)) calls "a deep-lying centre-forward position, occupied by Nándor Hidegkuti".

England "lined up in its usual WM formation", in which the centre-half traditionally marked "whoever was wearing the number 9 shirt". Hidegkuti wore 9 but played in midfield, and the centre-half, Harry Johnston, was "unable to decide whether to man-mark the deep-lying Hidegkuti or to remain in position". Johnston was one of six England players never picked again, and England lost the return in Budapest 7-1 the following May. [FIFA's account](https://inside.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/1954switzerland/news/the-beginning-of-the-magyars-magic) quotes Ferenc Puskás describing Hidegkuti as "perfect for the role, sitting at the front of the midfield".

It was, in effect, an early false nine, and the role it beat was the old centre-half. The modern versions are compared in [types of striker explained](/guides/types-of-striker-explained).

## What became of the wing-halves and the wingers

Once the back line went to four, the half-back line had nowhere to stand. The 4-2-4 added a fourth defender and left a "relatively empty midfield" that relied on defenders who could "not only steal the ball, but also hold it, pass it or even run with it". In Italy's version of the 4-3-3, one of the two wing-halves was converted "to a libero (sweeper)".

The forwards went the same way. The outside-forwards became wingers, and in English football they were moved into midfield as the 4-4-2 rose in the 1960s. At the 1966 World Cup, England under Alf Ramsey won without natural wingers, a side nicknamed "The Wingless Wonders". The inside-forward lived on as the second striker, a role the positions reference says was "initially developed by the famous Hungary national team" of the late 1940s and mid-1950s, and as the [No.10, the enganche and the trequartista](/guides/number-10-enganche-trequartista).

By the early 1970s, with players lined up in fours rather than in rows of two, three and five, the old vocabulary had gone. "Half-back" fell out of use and "midfield" replaced it.

## What the old names still mean today

Three of them are still in everyday use, with new meanings:

- **Full-back** now means a wide defender in a back four, not the last man in front of the goalkeeper.
- **Centre-half** came to mean centre-back. The role itself, and its modern types, are covered in [centre-back explained](/guides/centre-back-role-explained).
- **Centre-forward** still means the central striker, usually wearing 9.

Half-back, wing-half, inside-forward and outside-left are history, but the numbers they wore are not: the 5 on a centre-back's shirt and the 4 and 6 on a midfielder's are the half-back line of the 1890s, still standing.
