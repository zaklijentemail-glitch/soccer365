The Champions League stopped using groups. If you last paid close attention when it was eight groups of four and the top two went through, the competition you are watching now works differently enough to be worth twenty minutes.

## One table, thirty-six teams

The old structure split 32 clubs into eight groups of four. Everyone played their three group opponents twice, the top two advanced, third dropped into the Europa League, fourth went home.

The current structure puts **36 clubs in a single table**. There are no groups at all. Every team appears in one ranking from 1st to 36th, and where you finish in that table decides everything that follows.

This is a Swiss-model league, a format borrowed from chess tournaments and esports. The defining feature is that everyone competes in one standings table while playing different opponents.

## Eight matches, eight different opponents

Each club plays **eight matches in the league phase**: four at home, four away, against eight different teams. Nobody plays anyone twice.

That last point is the biggest practical change. Under the old format you played the same three sides home and away, and a bad night could be avenged. Now every fixture is a one-off. Lose at home to someone and there is no return leg to put it right.

It also means the eight opponents you get are not the eight anyone else gets. Two clubs finishing on the same points may have played completely different schedules, one considerably harder than the other. That is the trade the format makes: more variety, less symmetry.

## How the draw works

Clubs are sorted into **four pots of nine**, ranked by UEFA coefficient, which is a measure of recent European performance.

Each team is then drawn **two opponents from each pot**. Eight matches, two from each of the four seeding tiers, home and away split one each within every pair.

There are constraints on top of that: clubs from the same country cannot be drawn against each other, and there is a limit on how many opponents from any single country one club can face. The combination of those rules with 36 teams produces a problem too tangled to run as a traditional draw with balls and bowls, so it is done by software, with the ceremony showing the results rather than generating them.

The upshot for a supporter is that you get two matches against elite opposition, two against strong opposition, and so on down the pots. Nobody gets an easy eight, and nobody gets eight impossible ones.

## What each finishing position gets you

This is where the format creates its real tension, because the table splits three ways.

**1st to 8th.** Straight into the round of 16. Two extra weeks of rest, two fewer matches in a crowded season, and no chance of an early exit.

**9th to 24th.** Into a two-legged knockout playoff. Sixteen teams, eight ties, eight survivors joining the top eight in the round of 16. Seeding within this round favours the higher finishers, who face teams from the bottom half of the bracket.

**25th to 36th.** Eliminated. European season over.

The gap between 8th and 9th is the sharpest line in the competition. Eighth place means a rest. Ninth means two extra knockout matches against a club that has just had to win to be there, at the busiest point of the domestic season. Clubs chase those top eight places hard in the final round of the league phase, and the closing night has multiple teams re-ranking against each other in real time.

## No more Europa League parachute

Under the old format, finishing third in your group dropped you into the Europa League knockout round. That safety net is gone.

Finish 25th or below and your European campaign is finished. There is nothing to fall into. This was deliberate, intended to make the lower half of the table matter rather than offering a consolation route that clubs sometimes preferred anyway.

## The knockout bracket is seeded

Once the round of 16 is set, positions from the league phase keep working.

Higher finishers are drawn against playoff winners rather than each other, and the better-ranked side plays the **second leg at home**. Finishing well in the league phase is worth something all the way to the later rounds, which is a change from the old system where topping a group bought you relatively little.

## Does the format actually work

Mixed, and worth being honest about.

What it does well: more clubs are still alive in the final round of matches, the dead rubber has largely disappeared, and you get a wider range of fixtures rather than the same three opponents twice.

What it does badly: the single table is genuinely hard to read, because a club on 13 points may have had a much easier eight games than a club on 12, and nothing in the table shows you that. It also added matches to a calendar that was already the main complaint of every manager and player in Europe.

The honest summary is that the format was designed to produce more games and more revenue, and it does. Whether it produces a better competition depends on whether you value the extra jeopardy in the closing rounds more than you mind the extra fixtures.

## The short version

- **36 teams, one table**, no groups.
- **Eight matches** against **eight different opponents**, four home, four away.
- Opponents drawn **two from each of four pots**, seeded by coefficient.
- **1st to 8th** go straight to the round of 16.
- **9th to 24th** play a two-legged playoff.
- **25th to 36th** are out, with **no Europa League parachute**.
- The knockout bracket stays **seeded by league-phase finish**.
