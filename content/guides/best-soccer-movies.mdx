**The best soccer movie of all time is Jafar Panahi's Offside (2006)**, an Iranian drama that holds a 94% score from 85 critics on Rotten Tomatoes and won the Silver Bear Grand Jury Prize at the Berlin Film Festival, with Tom Hooper's The Damned United and Stephen Chow's Shaolin Soccer close behind. Below are seven of the best films built around the game, ranked on how critics and festivals actually judged them, with the year, director and record behind each one.

## How the list is ranked

Two things, in this order:

1. **Critical reception.** The aggregate scores on Rotten Tomatoes and Metacritic, which pool hundreds of individual reviews rather than one person's taste.
2. **Recognition.** Festival prizes, industry awards and box-office records, used to separate films that sit close together on review scores.

Taste in films varies more than form in a back four, so this list leans on documented reception rather than vibes. A film with a weaker score can still be essential viewing, which is why Escape to Victory and Goal! The Dream Begins, the two lowest-scoring entries here, are still in the seven: one has a cast no football film has matched since, and the other was made with FIFA's cooperation to use real clubs and players.

## 1. Offside (Jafar Panahi, 2006)

A group of young Iranian women, barred by law from entering football stadiums, try to get into Tehran's Azadi Stadium for Iran's World Cup qualifier against Bahrain. One disguises herself as a boy, is caught, and ends up held in a rooftop holding pen with the other women caught doing the same thing, guarded by young soldiers who would rather be watching the match themselves.

Panahi shot it in Iran, where it is still banned from public screening. It won the Silver Bear Grand Jury Prize at the 2006 Berlin International Film Festival, was an official selection at the New York and Toronto festivals that year, and topped Slant Magazine critic Ed Gonzalez's list of the best films of 2007. ## 2. The Damned United (Tom Hooper, 2009)

Adapted from David Peace's largely fictionalised 2006 novel, the film covers Brian Clough's 44 days as Leeds United manager in 1974 and the rivalry he built with the club years earlier while managing Derby County. Michael Sheen plays Clough, with Timothy Spall as his assistant Peter Taylor; both performances were singled out by critics, and Sheen's turn carried the film to a Satellite Award nomination.

Reviewers treated it as closer to character study than history: it compresses and reorders real events, including the timing of transfer signings and the venue of a 1968 FA Cup tie. Roger Ebert gave it 3.5 out of 4 stars, and The Guardian's Peter Bradshaw gave it 4 out of 5.

## 3. Shaolin Soccer (Stephen Chow, 2001)

Stephen Chow directs and stars in a comedy that fuses martial arts with five-a-side football, built around a team of former kung fu masters trying to win a tournament. It grossed HK$60.7 million, making it the highest-grossing Hong Kong film of 2001 and, at the time, the biggest in the territory's history; Chow's own Kung Fu Hustle overtook it three years later.

It won Best Film and Best Director at the 21st Hong Kong Film Awards and has since become a cult favourite well beyond Hong Kong, helped by a US release that trimmed some of the slapstick but kept the football.

## 4. Bend It Like Beckham (Gurinder Chadha, 2002)

Gurinder Chadha's comedy-drama follows a British Sikh teenager who joins a local women's football team against her family's wishes, with Keira Knightley and Parminder Nagra as the two leads. It opened in the UK in April 2002 and in the US the following March, and went on to win the ESPY Award for Best Sports Movie and the GLAAD Media Award for Outstanding Film.

It was also nominated for a Golden Globe for Best Motion Picture, Musical or Comedy, and a BAFTA for Best British Film. Its 85% Rotten Tomatoes score comes from 157 reviews, more than any other film on this list, a sign of how widely it was seen.

## 5. Looking for Eric (Ken Loach, 2009)

Ken Loach's film pairs a struggling Manchester postman, played by Steve Evets, with a version of Eric Cantona playing himself, who turns up as a kind of imagined life coach. It premiered in competition at the Cannes Film Festival in May 2009 and won the Prize of the Ecumenical Jury there.

John Henshaw won Best Supporting Actor at the British Independent Film Awards for his part in it, and Evets picked up a European Film Award nomination for Best Actor. It is as much about a fan's relationship with a footballer as it is about football itself, which is why it plays differently to the other films on this list. ## 6. Escape to Victory (John Huston, 1981)

John Huston's prisoner-of-war film is built around a football match between Allied prisoners and a German team, with Sylvester Stallone and Michael Caine leading a cast that also includes Max von Sydow and Pelé, playing Corporal Luis Fernandez. Around them, Huston filled the pitch with real players: Bobby Moore, Osvaldo Ardiles, Kazimierz Deyna, Paul Van Himst and a clutch of Ipswich Town players, including John Wark and Russell Osman, with Ipswich's Kevin Beattie doubling for Caine in the football scenes.

Critics were split on the film as a whole. Stanley Kauffmann of The New Republic called it "uproariously stupid," while Leonard Maltin said it was redeemed by football scenes he called "rightly celebrated." It earned $27.5 million against a $12 million budget and was entered into the Moscow International Film Festival; more than four decades on, the cast list is still the reason it is remembered. ## 7. Goal! The Dream Begins (Danny Cannon, 2005)

Danny Cannon's film follows Santiago Muñez, a fictional amateur player and undocumented Mexican immigrant in Los Angeles, as he gets a trial at Newcastle United. FIFA's cooperation let the production use real teams and players under licence, and Alan Shearer, Zinedine Zidane and Kieron Dyer all appear as themselves.

It is the lowest-scoring film on this list, with a 42% Rotten Tomatoes rating from 83 reviews, and critics such as Variety called it "a slickly mounted slice of can-do nonsense." Even so, Roger Ebert gave it three stars and called it "surprisingly effective," and the access it had to a real Premier League club and real internationals is still unusual for a football drama. It was the first film in a trilogy, grossing $27.6 million against a $33 million budget.
