**A centre-back is the defender who plays in the middle of the back line, and their job is to protect the space in front of goal: mark the centre-forward, win the ball in the air and on the ground, and organise the defenders around them.** In the modern game the same player is also expected to start attacks, carrying the ball forward and passing through the opponent's press.

Most teams play two centre-backs in a back four or three in a back three. The role changes with the system, but the core never does: if the centre-backs are beaten, there is usually only the goalkeeper left.

## Where the position came from

The older name, centre-half, is a clue that the position was not always a defensive one. What made it one was a change to the offside law.

In the 1925 close season, [Arsenal's own history records](http://www.arsenal.com/news/why-7-0-loss-newcastle-led-our-golden-era), the offside rule was altered so that "only two defenders (it was previously three) needed to be between an attacker and the goal". Attacks got easier. In late September 1925 Herbert Chapman's Arsenal lost 7-0 at Newcastle United, and Charlie Buchan proposed that "a deep-lying centre-half should be deployed to guard the edge of the penalty area, rather than man-mark a centre-forward". Buchan also wanted that player, rather than the full-backs, to run the offside trap. Jack Butler was first asked to play it; later the system ran with Herbie Roberts at centre-half. The deep role, the club says, "quickly became known as centre-back". It became the heart of Chapman's WM formation.

The back four came later. Coaches' Voice, in its [explainer on the modern centre-back](https://learning.coachesvoice.com/cv/modern-centre-back-football-tactics-explained-van-dijk-ramos-dias-bonucci/), says the line of three defenders in the W-M became four when a half-back dropped into it, and that "the idea is thought to have originated in Brazil, in response to their 1950 World Cup final defeat to Uruguay". That is where the familiar pair of centre-backs between two full-backs comes from.

## What a centre-back does

### Without the ball

This is still the job description. Coaches' Voice lists the defensive work as "organising the defensive line, communicating with teammates and taking a lead at defensive set-pieces", along with defending central areas by blocking shots, tackling, intercepting and clearing.

Two more duties have grown in importance. Because modern full-backs push so far forward, "modern centre-backs need to be agile enough to provide cover in wide areas". And because centre-backs work in pairs or threes, each has to know what the other is doing. FIFA's Technical Study Group, reviewing the [2023 Under-17 World Cup](https://fifatrainingcentre.com/en/game/tournaments/fu17wc/2023/post-tournament-analysis/strong-centre-back-partnerships.php), found the best pairs "were close [to each other] and connected, often working in the penalty area within six metres of each other", with "an acute awareness of their partner's role as well as their own". Its summary of what a partnership needs: "good communication, spatial awareness, fast decision-making and trust."

### With the ball

Here is what has changed most. Coaches' Voice says modern centre-backs "are often set up to get on the ball in numerically overloaded situations against opposing centre-forwards", and that "one key responsibility, then, is to drive forward with the ball when they have space to do so". They are "responsible for breaking lines with passes", and "switches of play or longer passes are also an important part of the modern centre-back's game".

A law change pushed this along. Since the 2019-20 season a goal kick is in play as soon as it "is kicked and clearly moves", under [Law 16](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/the-goal-kick/), while "opponents must be outside the penalty area until the ball is in play". That means, as Coaches' Voice notes, "centre-backs who are good on the ball can also now receive a pass from their goalkeeper inside their own penalty area".

## The types of centre-back

These are labels, not fixed positions, and most centre-backs mix two or three of them. But they describe real differences in how players and coaches approach the job.

| Type | Main job | Where it shows up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stopper | Steps out to engage the attacker early, before the ball reaches the danger area | One half of a back-four pair, often the more aggressive |
| Cover | Stays deeper, reads the play and sweeps up when the stopper is beaten | The other half of the pair |
| Ball-playing | Carries the ball out and breaks lines with passes | Any back line; now expected of almost every top centre-back |
| Libero (sweeper) | Plays free behind the defensive line, steps into midfield with the ball | Largely gone as a separate position |
| Wide centre-back | Covers the channel, carries the ball into midfield, overlaps | The two outside players in a back three |
| Centre-back at full-back | Defends wide but tucks in to form a back three in possession | A trend of the 2023-24 Premier League season |

### Stopper and cover

The classic pairing is one defender who attacks the ball and one who protects the space behind. FIFA's Technical Study Group, looking at centre-backs at the [2023 Under-20 World Cup](https://fifatrainingcentre.com/en/game/tournaments/2023-u20-fwc/centre-backs-defending-on-front-foot.php), put the case for stepping out: "As a centre-back, you are much happier defending when the ball is in front of you than when it is behind you." It also set out the condition that makes it safe: "The player committing to the challenge needs to trust that the cover and support is occurring behind him." And: "If one defender goes in, others around him have to cover." That is the stopper and the cover defender in two sentences.

### The libero

Libero is "taken from the Italian word for 'free'", as the Associated Press put it in its [obituary of Franz Beckenbauer](https://www.wsls.com/sports/2024/01/08/franz-beckenbauer-was-a-graceful-and-visionary-libero-who-changed-the-face-of-soccer/), and describes a player with "a covering role behind a defensive line". The idea was not new by the late 1960s, but Beckenbauer, a central midfielder in the 1966 World Cup final, "brought an attacking element to the deepest outfield position on the pitch". At Bayern Munich he was one of two nominal centre-backs, used as a libero "behind a three-man line", and would pick his moment to step out into midfield or start attacks with long passes.

It worked. Beckenbauer won the Ballon d'Or in 1972 and 1976, three straight German titles from 1972 to 1974, three straight European Cups from 1974 to 1976, and captained West Germany to the 1974 World Cup. The AP's verdict is that the role "has disappeared from the game" but "lives on in ball-playing center backs" in back fours, naming David Alaba at Real Madrid and, before him, Rio Ferdinand at Manchester United.

### The wide centre-back

In a back three the two outside centre-backs have a different job from the one in the middle. The Premier League's [3-4-3 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4244194) says "the two wider centre-backs may also push forward with runs on the inside of the wing-backs" and "often have space to dribble forwards", while without the ball they cover central spaces and track runs in behind. Gareth Southgate's England, it notes, often used Kyle Walker, a right-back by trade, as the right-sided centre-back. The shape is covered in full in our guides to the [3-4-3](/guides/3-4-3-formation-explained) and the [3-5-2](/guides/3-5-2-formation-explained).

### Centre-backs at full-back

The newest variation runs the other way. The Premier League's analysis [Are traditional full-backs becoming extinct?](https://premierleague.com/news/3971686) found head coaches "opting to play an increasing number of central defenders at full-back" in 2023-24, naming Ben White and Jakub Kiwior at Arsenal, Ezri Konsa at Aston Villa, and Nathan Aké, Joško Gvardiol and Manuel Akanji at Manchester City. Its explanation: today's defenders "are quicker, more mobile, less reliant on physical strength" and "a lot more proficient with their distribution". How that differs from a traditional full-back is set out in our guide to [full-backs, wing-backs and inverted full-backs](/guides/fullback-vs-wingback-vs-inverted-fullback).

## What makes a great centre-back

Coaches' Voice's portraits of four modern examples make a useful checklist, because each player is singled out for something different:

- **Leadership and organisation.** "Van Dijk is a commanding centre-back," and "a leader on the pitch, regularly communicating with and organising his teammates."
- **Reading the game.** Rúben Dias "reads the game very well and makes frequent interceptions thanks to his exceptional anticipation."
- **Duelling and passing range.** Sergio Ramos "has always shown impressive ability in one-on-one duels", and on the ball "is a bold passer with the ability to go short or long".
- **Doing all of it.** "Playing mostly as the middle centre-back in a back three, Bonucci is almost the definitive modern centre-back."

To that list FIFA's study groups add the partnership skills above: staying connected to the other centre-back, communicating, and choosing the right moment to step out. A great individual defender in a disconnected pair still leaves gaps.

The market has caught up with how much the position matters. In December 2017 Southampton agreed a fee with Liverpool for Van Dijk that would "set a new world-record for a defender", [Agence France-Presse reported](https://www.citizen.co.za/sport/soccer/world-soccer/fbl-eng-pr-liverpool-southampton-vandijk/), at a reported £75 million, above the £54 million Manchester City had paid Tottenham for Kyle Walker.

## Records and landmarks

| Landmark | Player | Figure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Most Premier League goals by a defender | John Terry | 41 |
| Most Premier League clean sheets by a defender | John Terry | 214 |
| Most Premier League titles as captain | John Terry | 5 |
| Ballon d'Or wins by a centre-back | Franz Beckenbauer | 2 (1972, 1976) |
| 2019 Ballon d'Or runner-up | Virgil van Dijk | 679 points to Messi's 686 |

Terry's numbers come from the [Premier League's Hall of Fame profile](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/3972366): 41 goals, "more than any other defender", and 214 clean sheets, "the most of any defender in Premier League history", across a Chelsea career that ran from December 1998 to May 2017. Next on the goals list are David Unsworth on 38, Leighton Baines on 32, and Ian Harte and Gary Cahill on 28 each.

Centre-backs rarely win the biggest individual prize. Sports Illustrated, [writing in 2019](https://www.si.com/soccer/2019/06/03/virgil-van-dijk-best-chance-defender-win-ballon-dor-fabio-cannavaro), counted Beckenbauer and Fabio Cannavaro, who won in 2006 after captaining Italy to the World Cup, as the only two centre-backs ever to win the Ballon d'Or. That December Van Dijk came closest to joining them: The Independent's [report of the result](https://uk.sports.yahoo.com/news/ballon-d-2019-result-sadio-103200050.html) had Lionel Messi on 686 points and Van Dijk on 679, which would have made him the fourth defender to win after Beckenbauer, Matthias Sammer and Cannavaro.
