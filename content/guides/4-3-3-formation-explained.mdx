**The 4-3-3 is a formation with four defenders, three central midfielders and three forwards, usually with one holding midfielder screening the back four and two wingers pushed up level with the centre-forward.** Its defining features are the midfield three, which gives a team an extra body in the centre against a two-man midfield, and the front three, which is the side's first line of defence as well as its main attacking threat.

The Premier League's tactics explainer, produced with Coaches' Voice, opens by saying the 4-3-3 "has been in vogue for some time now", and names Mikel Arteta's Arsenal, Xavi Hernández's Barcelona and Jürgen Klopp's Liverpool among its best-known exponents.

## Where it came from

According to the [Premier League's 4-1-4-1 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4243851), "the first real use of a 4-3-3 is often considered to be when Brazil's 1958 World Cup winners converted their 4-2-4 system into a 4-3-3 shape as they attacked". The 4-2-4 is also the parent of the [4-4-2](/guides/4-4-2-formation-explained): pull one forward back into midfield and you have a 4-3-3; pull both wingers back and you have a 4-4-2.

The shape's most famous early side is Ajax. [UEFA's profile of Ajax 1971-73](https://www.uefa.com/uefachampionsleague/news/newsid=2253548.html) describes a 4-3-3 with wingers, built by Rinus Michels and carried on by Stefan Kovács, with quick passing, heavy pressing, a coordinated offside trap and defenders who joined attacks. It was the team of Johan Cruyff, Johan Neeskens and the left winger Piet Keizer, and it won three European Cups in a row.

## The shape, line by line

**The back four.** Two centre-backs and two full-backs. The Premier League explainer asks the goalkeeper and defenders to be patient in build-up, drawing the opposition's press so that passing lanes open up into midfield. The full-backs then supply much of the width, which is why the 4-3-3 suits the attacking full-backs described in our guide to [full-backs, wing-backs and inverted full-backs](/guides/fullback-vs-wingback-vs-inverted-fullback).

**The holding midfielder.** The single pivot sits closer to the defensive line than the other two midfielders. The explainer uses Sergio Busquets as its model: "His movements in front of the centre-backs generated space for both the full-backs and inside players."

**The two No.8s.** The explainer asks all three midfielders for excellent technique, to offer passing lanes, to make diagonal runs and to work in congested central areas. Depending on the coach they can be box-to-box runners, the type covered in our [box-to-box midfielder guide](/guides/box-to-box-midfielder-explained), or small playmakers working in the half-spaces.

**The front three.** A centre-forward and two wide forwards. The explainer is clear that these have to be genuinely attacking players: "If these positions are played by midfielders or number eights, the 4-3-3 will carry less attacking threat." The striker can be a target man, a poacher or a false nine, the options set out in our guide to [types of striker](/guides/types-of-striker-explained).

## What each player is asked to do

| Position | With the ball | Without the ball |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goalkeeper | Plays patiently out from the back | Organises the back four |
| Centre-backs | Draw the press, pass into midfield | Mark attackers inside the box |
| Full-backs | Provide width, overlap or underlap the winger | Defend wide, with a midfielder sliding across to help |
| Holding midfielder | Drops near the centre-backs to receive and switch play | Protects the space between the lines |
| No.8s | Offer passing lanes, make diagonal runs | Shuffle across the pitch, support the full-backs |
| Wingers | Stretch the pitch, go one-on-one, cut inside | Press the build-up or drop into a midfield line |
| Centre-forward | Occupies the centre-backs, links play | Leads the press |

## How it changes without the ball

A 4-3-3 has two ways to defend. The first is to press high with all three forwards. The Premier League explainer says the attacking players, "as the first line of defence, they must press aggressively against the build-up", while the midfield stays close to the ball to stop counter-attacks and harries the opposition straight after losing possession.

The second is to drop into a block. The [4-1-4-1 explainer](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4243851) notes that the two shapes are close relatives: both have a back four and one defensive midfielder, but "with a 4-3-3, however, there are two wingers who attack much higher". When the wingers drop back in line with the No.8s, the team defends in a 4-1-4-1, and "naturally, the 4-1-4-1 often becomes a defensive component of the 4-3-3". In that block, "the defensive midfielder protects the spaces between the lines".

<Formation shape="4-1-4-1" roles="GK,LB,CB,CB,RB,DM,LM,CM,CM,RM,ST" caption="The same eleven without the ball: the wingers drop in line with the No.8s and the side defends in a 4-1-4-1" />

The midfield three does the most work here. "The three midfielders must therefore be switched on and adept at shuffling across the width of the pitch," the explainer says, citing Jordan Henderson at Liverpool, whose "support of his full-back was vital in allowing the team's centre-backs to mark inside the box".

## Strengths

**A midfield three.** Against a side with two central midfielders, such as a flat 4-4-2, the 4-3-3 always has a spare player in the middle of the pitch. Mourinho's Chelsea are the textbook case: the [Premier League's review of their 2004-05 season](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/2737934) says that with Claude Makelele as the sole pivot and a midfield trio in place, Chelsea regularly outnumbered rival teams in that area.

**Width without wing-backs.** Wingers and full-backs combine on each flank. The explainer describes two options: play wingers on the side of their dominant foot to create more one-on-ones, or play them inverted, on the opposite side, and let the full-back overlap to create two-against-one situations.

**Pressing from the front.** Three forwards can engage both centre-backs and a full-back at once, and the midfield three sits close behind them to win the second ball.

**Built-in counter-attacks.** With three forwards already high when the ball is won, the 4-3-3 can break at once. The explainer points to Vinícius Júnior's "pace in launching Real Madrid's counter-attacks" under Carlo Ancelotti.

## Weaknesses

**A beaten press.** The explainer admits the obvious risk: "The 4-3-3 is a system, with its three attacking players, in which the first line of the press can be vulnerable." Pass through the front three and the opponent can run at a midfield that has to cover the full width.

**Outnumbered up front on the break.** When a 4-3-3 counter-attacks, its forwards "will be in a 3v4 underload, or a 3v3", which is why the shape depends on quick, direct wide players.

**One holder, two jobs.** A single pivot has to start attacks and protect the back four. If an opposing No.10 sits on him, or two midfielders crowd him, the team's build-up and its defensive screen both suffer.

**Running in midfield.** With the wingers high, the three midfielders cover the gaps on both sides of the pitch. That demands endurance, and tired No.8s are usually the first players to be replaced.

## The teams that made it famous

| Team | Seasons | What it won |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Michels' and Kovács' Ajax | 1970-71 to 1972-73 | European Cup in 1971, 1972 and 1973 |
| José Mourinho's Chelsea | 2004-05 | Premier League with 95 points and 15 goals conceded |
| Pep Guardiola's Barcelona | 2008-09 | La Liga, Copa del Rey and Champions League |
| Jürgen Klopp's Liverpool | 2019-20 | Premier League with a club-record 99 points |

**Ajax.** UEFA records that Ajax beat Panathinaikos 2-0 in London in 1971, Inter 2-0 in Rotterdam in 1972, with Cruyff scoring both, and Juventus 1-0 in Belgrade in 1973. Michels won the first; Kovács, who took over when Michels left for Barcelona, won the other two.

**Mourinho's Chelsea.** The Premier League's review says Mourinho started 2004-05 with a narrow 4-4-2 diamond but "quickly changed to a 4-3-3". Makelele protected John Terry and Ricardo Carvalho, Frank Lampard played "a slightly more advanced box-to-box role" and scored 13 goals with a division-leading 18 assists, and Arjen Robben and Damien Duff attacked from the flanks. Chelsea lost once, took 95 points and conceded 15 goals, a Premier League record, with 25 clean sheets.

**Guardiola's Barcelona.** Busquets was, as [Goal's account of the era](https://www.goal.com/en-in/news/how-guardiola-turned-barcelona-and-messi-into-the-best-in/blt7bf6729a7e96959a) puts it, "a deep-lying defensive pivot", with Xavi and Iniesta "forming a wonderful tandem" ahead of him, and Guardiola later moved Lionel Messi into the middle as a false nine. In his first season the club became, in [UEFA's words](https://www.uefa.com/uefachampionsleague/news/01f3-0e78cf0b1cde-767a3469b6c3-1000--2008-09-fc-barcelona-2-0-manchester-united-fc-report), "the first Spanish club to complete the treble", beating Manchester United 2-0 in Rome with goals from Samuel Eto'o and Messi.

**Klopp's Liverpool.** The Premier League explainer lists Klopp's Liverpool among the 4-3-3's leading exponents. In 2019-20 the side won the club's first top-flight title in 30 years with a club-record 99 points, and [Liverpool's own review](https://www.liverpoolfc.com/news/first-team/403381-premier-league-champions-liverpool-s-class-of-2019-20) records the front three's league output: Mohamed Salah 19 goals and 10 assists, Sadio Mané 18 and seven, and Roberto Firmino nine and eight while playing every game. Trent Alexander-Arnold's 13 assists set a Premier League record for a defender.

## Variations of the 4-3-3

| Version | Midfield | Front line | Best at | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single-pivot 4-3-3 | One holder, two No.8s | Striker between two wingers | Controlling the centre, pressing high | Holder overloaded |
| False-nine 4-3-3 | One holder, two No.8s | Centre-forward drops into midfield | Overloading central midfield | Little presence in the box |
| Two free No.8s | Two small playmakers either side of a powerful holder | Wide forwards | Creating chances from the half-spaces | Less physical cover in midfield |
| 4-1-4-1 | One holder, four across | Lone striker | Compact defending | Fewer players high to counter |

The third row is Guardiola's Manchester City. The Premier League's [account of his innovations](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4663968) describes a 4-3-3 "built on two small, nimble No 8s shuttling up and down as playmakers either side of a powerful defensive midfielder", with David Silva, Bernardo Silva and İlkay Gündoğan in roles that did "not conform to the ideas of a British No 8".

## 4-3-3 against the other common shapes

| Opponent | Where the 4-3-3 has the edge | Where it is exposed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4-4-2 | Three central midfielders against two | Two strikers occupy both centre-backs |
| 4-2-3-1 | Three forwards can press the centre-backs and full-backs high | The No.10 can press the single holding midfielder |
| 3-5-2 | Three forwards can press the back three man-for-man | Two strikers occupy the two centre-backs; wing-backs pin the full-backs back |
| 4-3-3 (mirror) | Evenly matched across the pitch | Games often decided by which front three presses better |

The rows match the tables in our [4-4-2](/guides/4-4-2-formation-explained), [4-2-3-1](/guides/4-2-3-1-formation-explained) and [3-5-2](/guides/3-5-2-formation-explained) guides, seen from the other side.

## Is the 4-3-3 losing ground?

In the Premier League, yes. The league's [review of 2023-24 formations](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4030093) called "a pretty major drop in the use of 4-3-3" the headline news of that season: 222 uses in 2022-23 fell to 172, and the review said "playing a sole defensive lynchpin flanked by a pair of box-to-box midfielders in a 4-3-3 is becoming less desirable". Arsenal, Crystal Palace, Liverpool and Newcastle were the clubs that still used it as their main shape.

The slide continued. The [2024-25 review](https://www.premierleague.com/news/4323030) counted 114 uses, saying "the usually popular 4-3-3 has gone a little out of style", while the 4-2-3-1 was used 408 times and became the primary shape of 13 of the 20 clubs. Arteta, Eddie Howe and Ange Postecoglou were the coaches still using the 4-3-3. Arteta's Arsenal went on to win the 2025-26 title, [the club's first in 22 years](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4662306/arsenal-win-2025-26-premier-league-title-ending-22-year-wait-to-be-crowned-champions-again).

The trend is away from one holding midfielder towards two. A 4-2-3-1 keeps a double pivot in front of the defence, and the gap between the two shapes is often only where one midfielder stands.

## When a coach picks it

A 4-3-3 suits a squad with a holding midfielder who is calm on the ball under pressure, two midfielders with the legs to cover the width, and wide forwards with pace and the willingness to press. It suits a coach who wants to win the ball high and keep it, as Guardiola and Klopp did.

It suits a squad less when there is no natural holder, or when the opponent is set up to play through the front three and attack the space behind high full-backs. In that case many coaches now add a second holding midfielder, which is exactly how the 4-2-3-1 overtook it.
