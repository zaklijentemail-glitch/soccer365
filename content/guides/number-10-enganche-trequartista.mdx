**The No.10, the enganche and the trequartista are three names for the same zone: the creative player who works between the opponent's midfield and defence.** No.10 is the shirt traditionally worn there. Enganche, Argentine for "hook", is the fixed version, a playmaker who stays put and links midfield to attack. Trequartista, Italian for roughly "three-quarter", is the freer version, who roams across the front line looking for space.

In practice the labels overlap, and the same player gets called all three. But the differences are real, and they explain why the role once ran whole teams and why most coaches now ask it to do much more than pass.

## One zone, three names

[Coaches' Voice](https://learning.coachesvoice.com/cv/number-10-football-tactics-explained-bruno-fernandes-ozil-dybala-muller/) defines the No.10 as an attacker who operates in the spaces between central midfield and the front line, often receiving the ball "between the lines". It notes that the English name simply comes from the shirt number traditionally worn in that position, while Argentina called the role the enganche, the hook linking midfield to attack.

The Italian word describes a place rather than a number. The [Treccani dictionary](https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/trequartista/) derives trequartista from *tre quarti*, three quarters, and defines it as a player who acts near the opponent's penalty area, "roughly three-quarters of the way up the pitch", whose main attacking task is assisting the strikers.

Where the terms split is freedom. [Guide to Football](https://guidetofootball.com/tactics/playmaker-roles/) draws the line neatly: the enganche is "a classic number 10 who acts as a stationary creative pivot", and "where the trequartista roams, the enganche stays put". The advanced playmaker sits behind the centre-forward with at least some pressing duty; the trequartista is the more extreme version with less.

## The enganche: Argentina's hook

In Argentina, [Planet Football](https://www.planetfootball.com/nostalgia/tribute-juan-roman-riquelme-one-footballs-best-truest-no-10s) writes, the playmaker is called the enganche and is "revered there like nowhere else". The lineage runs through the No.10 shirt.

**Ricardo Bochini** is the purest club example. He played his whole career for Independiente, from his debut on 23 June 1972 to 1991, wearing No.10 and making 714 appearances with 108 goals. [Athletic Club's One Club Man dossier](https://cdn.athletic-club.eus/uploads/2022/03/OCMWA2022-Bochini-CAS.pdf) on him lists four Copa Libertadores titles (1973, 1974, 1975 and 1984) and two Intercontinental Cups (1973 and 1984), and calls him the best No.10 in Argentine history if you set aside Maradona and Messi. His speciality was the pass for a team-mate to finish: Argentine football still calls a precise ball that leaves a striker one-on-one with the goalkeeper a *pase bochinesco*. Maradona, the same document says, idolised him.

**Diego Maradona** is the archetype. At the 1986 World Cup, Carlos Bilardo built his side to free him. Bilardo told [Jonathan Wilson for Sports Illustrated](https://www.si.com/more-sports/2010/05/27/argentina-1986) that his plan was "three defenders, five midfielders and two forwards", a system practised for two years, and that against England "the extra man in midfield will give Maradona more room". Maradona played as a nominal second striker with freedom to roam. After his suspension at the 1994 World Cup, [90min notes](https://www.90min.com/es/posts/los-diez-mejores-10-de-la-historia-de-la-seleccion-argentina), Ariel Ortega inherited Argentina's No.10 and wore it for eight years; Pablo Aimar alternated the shirt with Juan Román Riquelme.

**Juan Román Riquelme** is the modern reference. Planet Football describes him as "arguably the purest example of the No.10" and "a throwback to a slower-paced and less physically demanding era". He made his Boca Juniors debut on 25 October 1997, replacing Maradona at half-time in a 2-1 win over River Plate. His career shows the role's strengths and its problem. At Barcelona, Louis van Gaal complained that Riquelme had been imposed on him and often used him on the wing. At Villarreal he helped the club finish third in 2004-05, its highest finish, and reach the Champions League semi-finals the next season, where Jens Lehmann saved his 88th-minute penalty against Arsenal. At the 2006 World Cup he set up Argentina's opener against Germany in the quarter-final, was withdrawn, and Germany equalised within eight minutes and won on penalties.

## The trequartista: Italy's free spirit

Among the theses written for the Italian federation's master course at Coverciano in 1999-2000 is one titled simply "Il trequartista", by the coach [Serse Cosmi](https://figc.dev/FigcLegacyAssets/SettoreTecnico/allegati/1572010104319.pdf). It is one of the clearest accounts of the role from inside the Italian game.

Cosmi describes the trequartista as the midfielder positioned on the opponent's three-quarter line, the player football legend "has often identified with the No.10". He lists the qualities: an important technical repertoire, excellent dribbling and final pass, a goalscorer too, a set-piece specialist, instinctive, creative and imaginative. He traces the line from Valentino Mazzola in the 1940s through Schiaffino, Puskás, Pelé, Mario Corso, Sandro Mazzola and Johan Cruyff, to Michel Platini, three Ballons d'Or in a row from 1983 to 1985, and Maradona. Then he names Roberto Baggio, Zico, Del Piero, Totti, Rivaldo, Rui Costa and Zidane as "the last exponents of the role in modern football".

The thesis is also precise about what happened to the role. Until the 1980s, Cosmi writes, the trequartista almost always played centrally behind two strikers, feeding them vertical passes and one-twos. Zonal marking and the 4-4-2 and 4-3-3 changed that, to the point of making him disappear or turning him into a second striker. By 1999-2000 he saw a return in the 4-3-1-2, but with a wider remit: a "dynamic playmaker" in the manner of Zidane, covering more ground and joining the defensive phase. Cosmi himself moved from a 4-3-1-2, to a 4-4-2 without a trequartista, and back to one in a 3-4-1-2.

## Why the classic No.10 faded

Cosmi's diagnosis, written for that 1999-2000 course, still holds. The zonal 4-4-2 and 4-3-3 that took over after the 1980s had no natural slot behind two strikers.

Then the space itself closed. Coaches' Voice notes that many teams now play defensive midfielders in the areas a classic No.10 would once have occupied. Omar Saleem, writing in [These Football Times](https://thesefootballtimes.co/2016/04/05/the-death-of-footballs-classic-number-10/), adds pace and pressing: holding midfielders denied the chance to play between the lines, and for pressing sides like Barcelona and Bayern there was "no time or space to carry passengers". In his account, creativity moved deeper, to midfielders such as Andrea Pirlo, Xabi Alonso and Marco Verratti dictating from in front of the back four, and wider, to inverted forwards.

The last obstacle was work rate. Guide to Football notes the trequartista sits less naturally in intense pressing systems, where "every attacking player is expected to help close passing lanes". A player who did not defend cost the team a man every time it lost the ball.

## Where the No.10 lives now

The role did not vanish; it was rewritten. Coaches' Voice places the modern No.10 behind a lone striker in a [4-2-3-1](/guides/4-2-3-1-formation-explained), with a double pivot behind, or behind two forwards in a 3-4-1-2.

The job description has grown. In possession, Coaches' Voice says, the modern No.10 does "far more than just creating for others": he runs into the box to support the centre-forward, drifts wide to help wingers and full-backs build overloads, and threatens goal by running beyond the striker. Out of possession, where the classic No.10 had "something of a free role", he now presses centre-backs, duels with full-backs and screens, and can "block access into the opposition's pivot".

Its examples show the range: Mesut Özil, praised for breaking lines with his passing; Thomas Müller, whose main strength is getting into scoring positions; Paulo Dybala, a dribbler in tight spaces; and Bruno Fernandes, who "finds runners in behind". The creative output of the best of them still stands out. Özil's 19 Premier League assists in 2015-16 and Bruno Fernandes's 21 in 2025-26 are both on [The Analyst's list](https://theanalyst.com/articles/most-assists-in-a-premier-league-season) of the competition's best single seasons, Fernandes's a new record that beat the 20 shared by Thierry Henry and Kevin De Bruyne. He added eight goals and was named [Premier League Player of the Season](https://www.beinsports.com/en-us/soccer/premier-league/articles/bruno-fernandes-breaks-historic-premier-league-assist-record-2026-05-24).

Some of what the classic No.10 did has also migrated to other positions. A [false 9](/guides/types-of-striker-explained) drops into the No.10's space from the front, and a [box-to-box midfielder](/guides/box-to-box-midfielder-explained) arrives in the box from deeper. Coaches' Voice notes that the decline of the role has led some coaches to question whether they need a specialist No.10 at all; the [football formations hub](/guides/football-formations-explained) compares the shapes that keep one and those that do not.

## Choosing between them

A coach who wants a fixed point to play through, with runners around him and two strikers to feed, is describing an enganche, and needs hard-working midfielders behind to cover; Cosmi wanted his two central midfielders physically strong and good at winning the ball back, not necessarily technical. A coach who wants a goal threat drifting across the front line is describing a trequartista. A coach who wants a creator who also leads the press is describing the modern No.10, and by Coaches' Voice's account that is the version the modern game demands.
