Offside is the rule that confuses new football fans the most. But the core idea is actually simple. This guide skips the technical language and explains it the easy way.

## The one picture to remember

Imagine the last defender (usually not the goalkeeper) standing on an invisible line across the pitch, level with where they're standing.

An attacking player is **offside** if, at the moment a teammate passes the ball to them, they are standing closer to the goal than that line.

That's it. That's the whole rule.

## Why does this rule exist?

Without it, an attacker could just stand right next to the goalkeeper the entire match, waiting for a teammate to launch the ball forward to them. That would make scoring too easy and the game boring to watch.

Offside forces attackers to time their runs, rather than simply camping out near the goal.

## Three things people get wrong

- **It's not about who is faster.** Offside has nothing to do with speed or who reaches the ball first. It only cares about where the player was standing at the exact moment the ball was passed.
- **Being level is fine.** If the attacker is exactly in line with the last defender, they are onside, not offside. Only being ahead of that defender counts.
- **It only matters when the ball is passed to you.** A player can stand in an offside position all day long. It only becomes an offense the moment a teammate actually plays the ball to them, and they get involved in the play.

## A simple example

A striker is standing near the opponent's goal. A teammate near the halfway line kicks the ball forward to them.

- If, at the moment of that kick, the striker was ahead of the last defender, it's offside. The referee stops the game.
- If the striker was level with or behind that last defender at that exact moment, it's onside. Play continues normally.

## Where VAR comes in

Because this decision depends on a split-second moment, it's often hard for a human referee's assistant to judge with the naked eye.
