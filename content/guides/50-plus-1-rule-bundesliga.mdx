**The 50+1 rule means a Bundesliga club cannot be bought: the members' club must always hold 50% of the votes in the company that runs its professional team, plus at least one more.** An investor can own shares, sometimes a great many of them, but the members keep the final say. A club whose investor takes control is not licensed to play in the Bundesliga or 2. Bundesliga.

The rule is one of the reasons German football looks so different from the Premier League. It has survived years of challenges from Hannover 96's long-time chief, a fan revolt over private equity and an eight-year competition-law review that ended in August 2026. It also has holes, and the regulator has now named them.

## What the rule actually says

German clubs began as registered associations, the eingetragener Verein or e.V., owned by their members. According to the [Federal Cartel Office's closing letter](https://www.bundeskartellamt.de/SharedDocs/Publikation/DE/Pressemitteilungen/50plus1_Schreiben_Verfahrenseinstellung.html), from the Bundesliga's creation until the 1998-99 season only clubs organised in that form could take part. A change to the German FA's statutes from the 1999-2000 season allowed companies to hold a licence for the first time, so that clubs could raise money on capital markets and bind sponsors into their structures.

The condition attached to that change is the 50+1 rule. Under section 8 of the DFL statutes, quoted in the same letter, a company can only be licensed if a club with its own football department holds a majority in it, and the parent club counts as a majority holder only if it has "50% of the voting shares plus at least one further vote" in the shareholders' meeting.

Three details matter:

- **Votes, not money.** The rule counts voting rights, not capital. An investor can put in most of the money and still be outvoted.
- **The KGaA route.** In a partnership limited by shares, the parent club can hold less than 50% of the votes if it, or a company it owns 100%, is the general partner with full powers to run the business.
- **No licence, no league.** The rule works through licensing. A company that breaks it cannot become a member of the league and cannot play.

## How the big clubs fit the rule

The two most successful German clubs show the two main models. These figures are from [bundesliga.com](https://www.bundesliga.com/en/bundesliga/news/50-1-fifty-plus-one-german-football-soccer-rule-explained-ownership-22832), as at March 2023.

| Club | Company | Who holds it | How 50+1 is met |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bayern Munich | FC Bayern München AG | Members' club 75%; Adidas, Allianz and Audi about 8.3% each | Members' club holds a plain majority |
| Borussia Dortmund | Borussia Dortmund GmbH & Co. KGaA | Members' club 4.61%; Bernd Geske 8.24%; Evonik 8.19%; Signal Iduna 5.98%; 72.27% traded on the stock market | Management company is 100% owned by the members' club |

Dortmund is the clearest example of how far the rule can stretch. Its members' club owns under 5% of the shares, yet controls the business, because the company that manages it belongs entirely to the members' club.

Bayern's members' club has more than 300,000 members, the largest of any sports club in the world, according to bundesliga.com. Dortmund's members' club had 168,163 in November 2022.

## The exceptions: Leverkusen and Wolfsburg

The statutes contained a way out, known in German as the Förderausnahme, or support exemption. The DFL Presidium could let another body take control of a club if it had supported the club's football "continuously and substantially" for more than 20 years. The backer had to go on funding amateur football at the same level, and could not sell its shares on except by handing them back free to the parent club, on pain of losing the licence.

Two clubs have it today.

**Bayer Leverkusen** was founded in 1904 by employees of Bayer, and **VfL Wolfsburg**, founded in 1945, has always been tied to Volkswagen. bundesliga.com notes that both have always been owned by their companies, long before they reached the Bundesliga.

**TSG Hoffenheim** was the third. The DFL granted Dietmar Hopp, who had funded the club for more than two decades, majority control in December 2014. In 2023 Hopp handed his voting majority back to the members' club, and Hoffenheim [completed its return to 50+1 in November 2023](https://www.getfootballnewsgermany.com/2023/hoffenheim-no-longer-exempt-from-the-501-rule/), leaving Leverkusen and Wolfsburg as the only exempt clubs. "It was never about power for me," Hopp said, [as reported by Inside World Football](https://www.insideworldfootball.com/2023/03/09/dfl-harden-501-rule-protect-german-clubs-investor-predators/).

The door has since closed. In March 2023 the DFL proposed that "there shall be no exemptions offered in the future from the 50+1 rule".

## The two clubs that test it

### Hannover 96 and Martin Kind

No one has fought the rule longer than Martin Kind, the businessman who ran Hannover 96. In 2009 he proposed scrapping it and 32 of the other 35 professional clubs voted against, according to bundesliga.com. He then applied for the support exemption himself in 2017. In July 2018 the DFL [rejected it](https://insideworldfootball.com/2018/07/20/hannover-96-vow-battle-overturn-incomprehensible-501-dfl-ruling), finding he had not invested enough over 20 years to qualify. Hannover called the decision "incomprehensible and obviously wrong". DFL president Reinhard Rauball said: "This decision was far from simple for the board."

Kind returned to the centre of the story in December 2023 (see below).

### RB Leipzig

Leipzig has no exemption. Red Bull took over the playing rights of fifth-tier Markranstädt in 2009 and rebranded the club, according to bundesliga.com. On paper the members' club holds the voting majority, but only a handful of people are eligible to vote, most of them linked to Red Bull.

The Cartel Office's letter does not name the club, but it describes the DFL treating as compliant a club that "over a long period consistently had only a few members with full voting rights", and first pressing it to open up, then not following through.

## The vote that exposed the gaps

In 2023 the DFL tried to sell a share of future media income to private equity. On 11 December 2023 the 36 clubs of the top two divisions voted in a secret ballot. The Cartel Office records that the motion passed with 24 of 36 votes, exactly the two-thirds majority the statutes required.

| Element | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Investor | CVC Capital Partners |
| Offer | About €1 billion up front |
| Price | Up to 8% of future Bundesliga broadcast revenue for 20 years |
| Vote | 24 of 36 clubs in favour, 11 December 2023 |
| Outcome | Dropped by the DFL in February 2024 after fan protests |

Hannover's members' club had told the DFL before the meeting that the club's managing director, Kind, must vote no. The DFL took no steps to check how he voted. [Inside World Football](https://www.insideworldfootball.com/2024/02/22/german-fan-protests-force-dfl-scrap-plans-raise-private-equity-cash-tv-rights/) reported that he was believed to have voted in favour, which he never confirmed. If he did, the motion passed only because the parent club's instruction was ignored, which is the opposite of what 50+1 is for.

Fans in both divisions protested for weeks, throwing tennis balls and chocolate coins onto pitches and sending remote-controlled cars and planes onto the field. The DFL dropped the deal. "Given current developments, a successful continuation of the process no longer seems possible," said Hans-Joachim Watzke, speaking for the DFL board.

## The Cartel Office ruling

The DFL itself asked the Federal Cartel Office, by letter of 18 July 2018, to confirm that it had no reason to act against the rule. The review lasted eight years and was reshaped by the European Court of Justice's December 2023 rulings in the Super League, ISU and Royal Antwerp cases.

The [final decision](https://www.bundeskartellamt.de/SharedDocs/Meldung/EN/Pressemitteilungen/2026/08_11_26_50Plus1.html), published on 12 August 2026, upheld the rule in principle. The office accepted that 50+1 restricts competition for investment, but found that keeping the club character of the sport and letting members take part can justify it, provided the rule is "applied consistently and without making objectively unjustified distinctions". Its president, Andreas Mundt, said: "This was not a proceeding to prohibit the 50+1 rule, and we have now also decided not to initiate one."

It named three problems:

1. **Membership access.** The DFL's licensing does not make sure every club gives fans real access to full voting membership.
2. **Voting.** The DFL did not apply the rule consistently in the December 2023 vote, when it ignored the instruction Hannover's members' club had given.
3. **The exemptions.** The DFL's offer to scrap the support exemption for the future but protect Leverkusen and Wolfsburg indefinitely was not enough under European case law. In the long run, the office said, a parent club open to new members should control the professional side at every club.

It set no deadline and said a longer transition period may be appropriate. In October 2026 the league said it would ask the 36 clubs to reaffirm 50+1 at its December assembly and bring final proposals [in the first half of 2027](https://www.getfootballnewsgermany.com/2026/bundesliga-501-2027-reform/). Since August 2026 the DFL has traded as Bundesliga e.V.

## The argument for and against

The case against comes from inside the game. Uli Hoeneß of Bayern has argued that German clubs are "falling behind internationally" and that individual clubs should decide whether to take outside money. The case for has its own voice at Dortmund: Watzke has said that most investors want to earn money, adding: "And where do they get it from? The spectators."

The Cartel Office weighed that argument with numbers. It noted that the 36 clubs of the top two divisions earned €6.33 billion between them in 2024-25, mostly from media rights, tickets and sponsorship, and that clubs under the rule, Bayern, Dortmund, Eintracht Frankfurt and Freiburg among them, have competed successfully in UEFA competitions, in some cases winning them.
