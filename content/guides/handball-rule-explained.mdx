Ask a stadium full of people whether something was handball and you will get two confident, opposite answers. This is the law that produces more argument than any other, including offside, and a lot of the argument comes from people applying a rule that has not existed for years.

## Start with what the law does not say

The most common belief about handball is that it comes down to intent: hand to ball is an offence, ball to hand is not.

That has not been the test for a long time. Intent is one route to an offence, and it is not the only one. A player who never meant to touch the ball can still concede a penalty, and a player who clearly moved an arm can occasionally get away with it if the referee judges the movement was not towards the ball.

Forget the ball-to-hand formula. It is a pub rule.

## Where the arm actually ends

[The Laws](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/fouls-and-misconduct/) draw the boundary at **the bottom of the armpit**.

Below that line is arm, and contact there can be an offence. Above it, meaning the shoulder and the very top of the upper arm, is not. This is why a replay will freeze on a frame and everyone will squint at whether the ball caught the shoulder or a couple of inches lower.

The line is arbitrary in the sense that any line would be, but it exists for a reason. A shoulder is part of the body you can legally use, and without a boundary every ball that hit a player anywhere on the upper body would be arguable.

## The two ways to commit handball

**Deliberately moving a hand or arm towards the ball.** This is the straightforward one and it is always an offence. The player reached, blocked, flicked, or grabbed. Intent is visible and nobody argues much about these.

**Making the body unnaturally bigger.** This is where the fights happen. The test is whether the arm's position was justified by what the player's body was doing at that moment. An arm out for balance while turning at speed is defensible. An arm raised above the head while jumping to block a cross is much harder to defend, because the arm added blocking area the player would not otherwise have had.

Notice that the second route says nothing about intent. A defender can be entirely surprised by the ball and still concede a penalty, because the question is not what he wanted, it is where his arm was and whether it needed to be there.

## What referees actually weigh

The written law is short. Applying it involves a set of judgements that are not evenly weighted and are never all present at once.

**Distance.** A ball struck from two yards away gives nobody time to move. The closer the shot, the more forgiving the reading, because reacting was not physically possible.

**Deflections.** If the ball comes off another player, or off the defender's own leg or head, and then hits his arm, that normally kills the offence. The arm position did not cause the contact.

**Body movement.** Sliding, jumping and sprinting all move arms in predictable ways. A referee is asking whether this arm position belongs to this movement.

**Direction of movement.** An arm travelling towards the ball reads very differently from an arm the ball travels towards.

None of these are tick-boxes and no two referees weigh them identically, which is exactly why two incidents that look the same on television get given differently.

## The attacker rule is stricter, and that surprises people

There is an asymmetry in the law that catches out a lot of viewers.

If a player scores with their hand or arm, the goal is disallowed **even if the contact was completely accidental**. No judgement about unnatural position, no consideration of distance. The ball touched the arm and went in, so it does not count.

The same applies to accidental handball by the scorer immediately before scoring. What does not apply is accidental handball by a teammate earlier in the move, or accidental handball by the scorer several passes back. The window is deliberately narrow, and it was tightened after a spell where goals were being ruled out for incidental contact deep in the build-up.

So a defender can concede a goal off his arm and it stands. An attacker cannot score off his and have it count. The reasoning is that a goal is the outcome the whole game is about, and it should not arrive by hand under any circumstances.

## Handball and VAR

Handball is the standard example of why VAR frustrates people, and it deserves the reputation.

Offside is a factual question. A player was ahead of the line or was not, and a still frame answers it. Handball is a judgement question, and no number of camera angles resolves a judgement. Slow motion actively distorts it, because a movement that took a fifth of a second looks deliberate and considered when stretched over three.

VAR can only intervene on a clear and obvious error. Since most handball decisions are arguable rather than clearly wrong, the video officials leave the original call alone far more often than viewers expect. That reads as the system refusing to do its job, when it is the system doing exactly what it was designed to do. Our guide to [how VAR works](/guides/what-is-var-and-how-does-it-work) covers what it can and cannot touch.

## The goalkeeper exception

Inside their own penalty area, a goalkeeper may handle the ball. Outside it they are an ordinary player, and a keeper who handles beyond the line commits handball like anyone else, usually with a card attached if it stops an attack.

There is a separate restriction that people file under handball but is really its own law: a goalkeeper cannot handle a ball deliberately kicked to them by a teammate. That is a back-pass offence and produces an indirect free kick rather than a penalty, even inside the area.

## Why it will keep changing

The handball law has been rewritten repeatedly, and each version fixes the previous complaint while creating a new one.

Tighten it, and soft penalties multiply for defenders who did nothing wrong. Loosen it, and defenders start blocking shots with arms they can now afford to leave out. Every version has been an attempt to sit between those, and none has held for long.

The honest position is that there is no wording that makes this rule feel fair in every case. It requires a human to decide whether an arm belonged where it was, and humans will disagree about that permanently.

## The short version

- The arm ends at **the bottom of the armpit**. Above it is not handball.
- Two routes to an offence: **deliberately moving the arm to the ball**, or **making the body unnaturally bigger**.
- Intent is not required for the second route.
- **Distance, deflections and body movement** all soften a call.
- An **attacker scoring off their arm** is always disallowed, accidental or not.
- VAR rarely overturns these, because they are judgements rather than facts.
