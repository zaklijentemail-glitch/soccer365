Yellow and red cards are how referees keep control of a match. Think of them as a two-step warning system: a yellow card is a caution that stays on the record, while a red card ends a player's afternoon on the spot.

That's the version everyone knows. The detail underneath it — which offences map to which card, what happens afterwards, and what can be challenged — is where most of the arguments actually happen.

## What earns a yellow card

The [Laws of the Game](https://www.theifab.com/laws/latest/fouls-and-misconduct/) set out a defined list of cautionable offences. A referee doesn't get to invent categories; he decides whether what he saw fits one.

- **Unsporting behaviour** — the broadest category, covering reckless fouls, simulation, handling to stop an attack, and celebrating in ways the Laws prohibit.
- **Dissent** by word or action.
- **Persistent offences.** No single foul deserves a card, but the accumulation does. This is the one players complain about most, because there's no fixed number at which it triggers.
- **Delaying the restart of play** — time-wasting.
- **Failing to respect the required distance** at a corner, free kick or throw-in.
- **Entering or re-entering the field without permission**, or deliberately leaving it without permission.

A caution is a formal warning with consequences attached. Commit a second cautionable offence in the same match and the referee has no discretion left.

## What earns a straight red card

Some offences skip the warning entirely:

- **Serious foul play** — a challenge using excessive force or endangering an opponent's safety.
- **Violent conduct** — striking or attempting to strike someone, whether or not the ball is anywhere nearby, and whether or not the target is an opponent.
- **Spitting** at anyone.
- **Denying an obvious goalscoring opportunity by handball.**
- **Denying an obvious goalscoring opportunity by a foul** — with an important exception, below.
- **Offensive, insulting or abusive language or gestures.**
- **Receiving a second caution** in the same match.

## The penalty-area exception people find confusing

You'll regularly see a defender concede a penalty for a clear foul on a striker clean through on goal — and receive only a yellow card. This looks like leniency. It isn't; it's a deliberate rule change.

The old position was known as **triple punishment**: the attacking team got a penalty, the defender was sent off, and he was then suspended for the next match. Three penalties for one offence, and it frequently ruined the game as a contest.

The Laws were amended so that where a defender denies a goalscoring opportunity **inside the penalty area** while making a **genuine attempt to play the ball**, and a penalty is awarded, the offence becomes a caution rather than a dismissal.

The exceptions to the exception matter:

- **Holding, pushing, pulling, or no attempt to play the ball** — still a red card.
- **Handball** to deny a goal or an obvious opportunity — still a red card.
- **Outside the penalty area** — still a red card, because there's no penalty compensating the attacking team.

So the question a referee is answering isn't "was it a foul?" but "was he genuinely trying to play the ball, in the box, with a penalty given?"

## Two yellows equal one red

If a player picks up a second caution in the same game, the referee shows the yellow and then the red. The player leaves the field.

The distinction from a straight red is what happens next. A dismissal for two cautions normally carries the minimum suspension, usually a single match. A straight red — particularly for violent conduct — can carry considerably more, and the length is set by the competition's disciplinary process rather than by the referee.

One asymmetry catches teams out badly: **VAR cannot intervene on a second yellow card.** The video assistant can review a straight red, and can recommend one the referee missed, but a second caution sits outside its remit. A player sent off for two soft yellows has no in-match remedy, while a player shown a straight red for the same incident would have had one. Our guide to [how VAR works](/guides/what-is-var-and-how-does-it-work) sets out exactly what the system can and cannot touch.

## What happens after the whistle

A sending-off has immediate consequences and delayed ones.

**Immediately:** the team plays on a player short. A red card cannot be replaced by a substitute — that's the point of it.

**Afterwards:** the player serves a suspension. Two yellows typically means one match. A straight red means at least one, and often more depending on the offence, with violent conduct carrying the heaviest bans. Competitions maintain their own disciplinary panels, which review footage and confirm, extend, or occasionally reduce the punishment.

Suspensions are also **competition-specific**. A ban picked up in a domestic cup does not automatically apply in the league, and vice versa. Bans in international competition follow their own rules again. This is why a player can be suspended on Saturday and available on Wednesday.

## Accumulation: the quiet suspension

Beyond sendings-off, most competitions suspend players who collect a set number of cautions across separate matches — commonly five in a league season, though the number and the deadline vary and are set by the competition, not by the Laws of the Game.

Two features are worth knowing:

- **The count resets at a defined point.** Tournaments usually wipe single yellows at a set stage, often after the group phase or the quarter-finals, so a player doesn't miss a final for a caution picked up months earlier.
- **The threshold usually tightens as the season progresses**, with fewer cards required to trigger a ban early on than late.

Managers plan around this. A player on four yellows in a title race gets rested for a fixture the team expects to win comfortably.

## Can a card be rescinded?

This is the question that produces the most confusion, because the answer is different for the two colours.

**Red cards: yes, they can be appealed.** A club can challenge a dismissal, and a disciplinary panel can overturn it, wiping the suspension. Appeals succeed most reliably where there's a factual error — mistaken identity, or an incident the panel can see was plainly not what the referee believed. Appeals against **judgement** — "it wasn't really excessive force" — succeed far less often, because panels are reluctant to substitute their opinion for the referee's on a subjective call. Many competitions also penalise **frivolous appeals** by extending the original ban, which is a real deterrent.

**Yellow cards: generally, no.** Individual cautions are usually not appealable at all. The reasoning is practical: thousands of yellows are shown every season, and a right of appeal against each one would collapse under its own weight.

There are narrow exceptions:

- **Mistaken identity.** If the referee booked the wrong player, that can be corrected, because it's a factual error rather than a judgement.
- **Simulation.** Some national associations operate a separate process for deceiving a match official, which can cut both ways — reviewing a card given for a dive, or punishing a player who won one.
- **Where the caution triggers a suspension**, some competitions allow a challenge to the suspension itself rather than the card.

Appeals against cards in international competition follow the governing body's own disciplinary code, which is why federations sometimes petition FIFA directly over a booking that would rule a player out of a knockout match — as in the case of France's appeal over Olise's yellow card and the wider Balogun ruling on card appeals.

## Cards for people who aren't players

Since 2019, referees have been able to show cards to **coaches and team officials** in the technical area. Before that a referee could only order someone from the touchline, with no formal record.

The offences mirror the player list adapted to the dugout: dissent, delaying a restart, entering the field, and abusive conduct. A dismissed official leaves the technical area entirely.

Where the offender cannot be identified — a coordinated protest from a bench, for instance — the senior coach present receives the card.

## The short version

- **Yellow** is a caution from a defined list, and two in a match means dismissal.
- **Red** is for a defined set of serious offences, and the team plays a man down.
- **A penalty-area foul** with a genuine attempt to play the ball is a caution, not a dismissal.
- **VAR** reaches straight reds, never second yellows.
- **Reds can be appealed; yellows almost never can**, outside mistaken identity.

The colour tells you what happened in the moment. The competition's disciplinary rules decide what it actually costs.
